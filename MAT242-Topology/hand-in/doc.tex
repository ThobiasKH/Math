\begin{prob*}[1]
    Let $X = [-1,1] \times \{-1,+1\} \subseteq \mathbb R^2$ have 
    the usual metric topology and define a relation 
    $\sim$ on $X$ saying that $(a,-1) \sim (a,1)$ for all 
    $a \in [-1,0) \cup (0,1]$. Let $Y = X /\sim$ have 
    the quotient topology 

    \begin{itemize}
        \item Check that $\sim$ is an equivalence relation 
            (nevermind I guess?) 
        \item Is $Y$ Hausdorff? 
        \item Does $Y$ have a countable basis for its 
            topology? 
        \item Check up the definition of connectedness in 
            section 23: is $Y$ connected? 
        \item Check up the definition of compact in section 
            $26$: is $Y$ compact? 
    \end{itemize}
\end{prob*}


\begin{proof}[Solution and proof of all]
    \medskip
    \

    \textbf{Hausdorff?}

    Recall that $Y$ is Hausdorff if 
    any two distinct points have some open neighborhoods 
    that are disjoint. 

    We will use the fact that open sets in 
    $X$ correspond exactly with open sets in $Y$.  

    For $Y$ to have the Hausdorff property
    we would require that, as 
    $(0,1)$ and $(0,-1)$ are distinct, 
    they have some open disjoint neighborhoods 
    that are disjoint. 

    Denote $(a,1) = a_+$ and $(0,-1) = a_-$. 
    Let $U$ be an open neighborhood of $0_+$ and 
    $V$ an open neighborhood of $0_-$. 

    Then $q^{-1}(U), q^{-1}(V)$ are open neighborhoods of $X$. 
    As $X$ has the metric topology we know there are 
    $\varepsilon_+, \varepsilon_- > 0$ such that 
    \[
        (-\varepsilon_+, \varepsilon_+) \times \{1\} 
        \subseteq q^{-1}(U)
    \]
    and 
    \[
        (-\varepsilon_-, \varepsilon_-) \times \{-1\} 
        \subseteq q^{-1}(V). 
    \]
    Choose $0 < a < \min\{\varepsilon_-, \varepsilon_+\}$. 
    Then $a_+ \in q^{-1}(U)$ and $a_- \in q^{-1}(V)$, but 
    \[
        a_+ \sim a_-
    \]
    so 
    \[
        q(a_+) = q(a_-) \in Y. 
    \]
    Call this point $y$. 
    Then $y \in U \cap V$, meaning they are not disjoint. 
    This violates the Hausdorff property.

    \textbf{Countable basis?} 

    Indeed it does have a countable basis. 

    $X$ has a countable basis since it is just 
    two copies of $[-1,1] \subseteq \mathbb R$ which 
    has a countable basis which we can construct using 
    the rationals. 

    Let $\mathcal B = \{B_1,B_2,\ldots\}$ be such a basis. 
    Since $q$ is open ($q^{-1}q(B)$ is open), 
    \[
        \mathcal C = \{q(B) \bigm| B \in \mathcal B\}
    \]
    consists of open sets in $Y$. Moreover, if 
    $y \in U$ for some open set in $Y$, choose 
    $x \in q^{-1}(U)$. Since $q^{-1}(U)$ is open, there 
    is some $B \in \mathcal B$ with 
    \[
        x \in B \subseteq q^{-1}(U). 
    \]
    Therefore $Y \in q(B) \subseteq U$. SO 
    $\mathcal C$ is a countable basis for $Y$. 

    \textbf{Connected?}
    
    The space is indeed connected. 
    I like to use the following procedure. 

    Let $f : Y \to \{0,1\}$ be any continuous function 
    where the codomain has the discrete topology. 

    Let $X_+ = [-1,1] \times \{1\}$ and 
    $X_- = [-1,1] \times \{-1\}$. 
    $f \circ q$ is continuous so the restrictions 
    \[
        f \circ q \bigm|_{X_+} : X_+ \to \{0,1\}
    \]
    and 
    \[
        f \circ q\bigm|_{X_-} : X_- \to \{0,1\}
    \]
    are continuous. $X_+$ and $X_-$ are homeomorphic to 
    $[-1,1]$ and therefore connected so
    these maps must be constant. 
    For convenience write  
    \[
        f \circ q\bigm|_{X_+} \equiv c_+, 
        \ \text{and} \ 
        f \circ q\bigm|_{X_-} \equiv c_- 
    \]
    where $c_+,c_- \in \{0,1\}$. 

    Recall that $X_+ \ni a_+ \sim a_- \in X_-$ so 
    $q(a_+) = q(a_-)$. Hence 
    \[
        c_+ = f(q(a_+)) = f(q(a_-)) = c_-. 
    \]

    As $Y = q(X_+) \cup q(X_-)$ the map $f$ must be constant
    on the whole domain. 

    Hence the space is 
    connected!

    \textbf{Compact?} 

    The space is indeed compact. 

    We know that $[-1,1] \times \{1\}, \ 
    [-1,1] \times \{-1\} \cong [-1,1]$ are compact. 
    So for a covering of $X$ reduce 
    the open covers on each copy of the line to a finite 
    cover. 

    Now take some open cover $\bigcup_{\alpha} U_\alpha$ 
    of $Y$. Then 
    \[
        q^{-1}\left(\bigcup_\alpha U_\alpha \right) 
        = \bigcup_\alpha q^{-1}(U_\alpha)
    \]
    is a cover of $X$. Since $X$ is compact we can 
    take a finite number of these 
    \[
        \bigcup_{\alpha_i, \ i \in [n]} q^{-1}(U_\alpha)
    \]
    to cover $X$. Meaning, every $a_+$ is contained in 
    some $U_{\alpha_i}$ and every $a_-$ is contained in 
    some $U_{\alpha_j}$. Then $q(a_+), \ q(a_-) \in 
    U_{\alpha_i} \cup U_{\alpha_j}$ so the image of this 
    finite cover suffices as a finite covering of $Y$. 
    
    It would also have sufficed to point out that 
    the continuous image of a compact space is 
    compact!
\end{proof}

\begin{prob*}[2]
    Let $\mathbb R^\omega$ be the countably infinite 
    product of $\mathbb R$ with itself. Let 
    $\mathbb R^\infty \subset \mathbb R^\omega$ be the 
    subset of all sequences $(x_1,x_2,\ldots)$ that 
    are eventually zero (i.e., there exists an 
    $N$ such that $x_n = 0$ for all $n > N$). 

    \begin{enumerate}[label=\alph*)]
        \item What is the interior and closure of $\mathbb R^\infty$ 
            in $\mathbb R^\omega$ if $\mathbb R^\omega$ is given the 
            product topology? Is $\mathbb R^\infty$ (with this topology) 
            Hausdorff?
        \item What is the interior and closure of 
            $\mathbb R^\infty$ in $\mathbb R^\omega$ if 
            $\mathbb R^\omega$ is given the box topology? 
            Is $\mathbb R^\infty$ (with this topology)
            Hausdorff? 
        \item What is the interior and closure of 
            $\mathbb R^\infty$ in $\mathbb R^\omega$ if 
            $\mathbb R^\omega$ is given the uniform topology? 
            Is $\mathbb R^\infty$ (with this topology)
            Hausdorff?  
    \end{enumerate}
\end{prob*}

\begin{proof}[Solution and proof of (a)]
    A basic open neighborhood 
    of $x = (x_1,x_2,\ldots)$ looks like 
    \[
        \left(\prod_{i \in [N]} U_i \right)
        \times \mathbb R \times \mathbb R \times \cdots, 
    \]
    so only finitely many coordinates are 
    restricted. 

    For the interior, take any $x \in \mathbb R^\infty$. 
    Any neighborhood of $x$leaves all sufficiently 
    large coordinates completely unrestriced. Hence 
    inside that neighboorhood we can find a sequence 
    $y$ with, e.g. $y_n = 1$ for infinitely many 
    unrestriced coordinates. 

    Then $y$ is not eventualyl zero. Therefore no open neighborhood 
    of $x$ lies entirely inside $\mathbb R^\infty$. 
    Hence the interior $\operatorname{Int}(\mathbb R^\infty) = 
    \emptyset$. 

    For closure, take an arbitrary $x = (x_1,x_2, \ldots) 
    \in \mathbb R^\omega$. 
    Take a neighborhood resttricting only 
    the first $N$ coordinates. 
    Choose $y = (x_1,\ldots,x_n, 0, 0, \ldots).$ 
    Then 
    \[
        y \in \mathbb R^\infty
    \]
    and $y$ lies in the given neighborhood of $x$. 
    Thus every open neighborhood of every 
    $x \in \mathbb R^\omega$ intersects $\mathbb R^\infty$. 
    So $\overline{\mathbb R^\infty} = \mathbb R^\omega$, 
    meaning $\mathbb R^\infty$ is dense in 
    $\mathbb R^\omega$ under the product topology.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Solution and proof of (b)]
    Now a neighborhood is much smaller 
    \[
        \prod_{n=1}^\infty U_n, 
    \]
    where every $U_n \subseteq \mathbb R$ is open. 

    Suppose $x \in \mathbb R^\infty$, so for 
    some $N$, $x_n = 0 \ (n > N)$. 
    
    Take any box neighborhood 
    \[
        \prod_{n=1}^\infty U_n 
    \]
    of $x$. 
    For every $n > N$, $0 \in U_n$. 
    Since $U_n$ is an open neighborhood of $0$, it contains 
    some nonzero $y_n$. Then the resulting sequence 
    lies in the box neighborhood, but is not eventually 
    zero. 
    Therefore the interior is empty. 

    Now suppose $x \not\in \mathbb R^\infty$. Then 
    $x_n \neq 0$ for infinitely many $n$. 
    Let $I = \{x \bigm| x_n \neq 0\}$. 

    For every $n \in I$, chjoose an open interval 
    $U_n$ around $x_n$ such that $0 \not\in U_n$. 
    For $n \not\in I$ just take $U_n = \mathbb R$. 
    Then 
    \[
        U = \prod_{n=1}^\infty U_n 
    \]
    is an open neighborhood of $x$. 

    Every $y \in U$ satisfies $y_n \neq 0$ for every 
    $n \in I$, and $I$ is infinite. Hence no 
    $y \in U$ is eventually zero. 

    So 
    \[
        U \cap \mathbb R^\infty = \emptyset. 
    \]
    Therefore every point outside $\mathbb R^\infty$ 
    has a neighborhood avoiding $\mathbb R^\infty$. 
    Thus $\overline{\mathbb R^\infty} = \mathbb R^\infty$. 
    So in the box topology it is closed, but still 
    has empty interior. 
\end{proof}

\begin{prob*}[3]
    Let $X$ be a topological space, $Y$ a set 
    and let $f: X \to Y$ be a surjection. 

    Show that the quotient topology on $Y$ is the 
    unique topology so that 
    \begin{flalign*}
        &\text{if} \ Z \ \text{is a topological space and}
        \ g : Y \to Z \ \text{is a function} \\ &\text{then}\ g 
        \ \text{is continuous if and only if} \ gf \ \text{is continuous}.
    \end{flalign*}
\end{prob*}

\begin{proof}
    With $f : X \to Y$ some surjection, the 
    quotient topology on $Y$ is the collection 
    \[
        \tau_f = \{U \subseteq Y \bigm| f^{-1}(U) \ \text{is 
        open in} \ X\}. 
    \]
    Equivalently, $U$ is open in $Y$ if and only if 
    its preimage under $f$ is open in $X$. 

    We first prove existence. 
    Assume $Y$ has the quotient topology and 
    let $g : Y \to Z$. 
    
    \[
        g \ \text{continuous} 
        \Rightarrow g \circ f \ \text{continuous}
    \]
    is immediate as $f$ is continuous and the 
    composition of continuous functions is continuous. 

    For the converse, suppose the composition $g \circ f$ is 
    continuous. Then for an open set in $U$ in $Z$ 
    the preimage 
    \[
        (g\circ f)^{-1}(U) = f^{-1}(g^{-1}(U)) \subseteq X 
    \]
    is open. Then, by assumption, 
    $g^{-1}(U) \subseteq Y$ is open. So the preimage of 
    open sets under $g$ is open, hence it is continuous.

    For uniqueness, suppose $\tau$ is some 
    other topology on $Y$ having the property. 

    Let $Z = (Y,\tau_f)$ and 
    $g = \operatorname{id}_{Y} : (Y,\tau) \to (Y,\tau_f)$. 
    Then 
    \[
        g \circ f = f : X \to (Y,\tau_f) 
    \]
    which is continuous. 

    Since $\tau$ has the stated property, $g$ is therefore continuous. Hence every $\tau_f$-open set is $\tau$-open, so \[ \tau_f\subseteq\tau. \] For the opposite inclusion, observe first that \[ f:X\to(Y,\tau) \] is continuous. Indeed, taking \[ g=\operatorname{id}_Y:(Y,\tau)\to(Y,\tau) \] in the assumed property, $g$ is continuous, and hence $g\circ f=f$ is continuous. Now consider \[ h=\operatorname{id}_Y:(Y,\tau_f)\to(Y,\tau). \] Since \[ h\circ f=f:X\to(Y,\tau) \] is continuous, the universal property already proved for the quotient topology implies that $h$ is continuous. Therefore every $\tau$-open set is $\tau_f$-open, and hence \[ \tau\subseteq\tau_f. \] Consequently, \[ \tau=\tau_f, \] so the quotient topology is unique.
\end{proof}

\begin{prob*}[Munkres 17.12]
    Show that a subspace of a Hausdorff space 
    is Hausdorff. 
\end{prob*}
\begin{proof}
    Let $X$ be a Hausdorff space and let 
    $A \subseteq X$ be a subspace, i.e. 
    having the topology 
    \[
        \tau_A = \{U \cap A \bigm| U \subseteq X \ \text{open}\}. 
    \]

    Take any two distinct 
    $x,y \in A$. Then there exist open 
    $U_x, \ U_y \subseteq X$ such that 
    $x \in U_x, \ y \in U_y$ and 
    $U_x \cap U_y = \emptyset$. 

    Then 
    \[
        A \cap U_x \subseteq U_x
    \]
    and 
    \[
        A \cap U_y \subseteq U_y
    \]
    with 
    \[
        (A \cap U_x) \cap (A \cap U_y) 
        = A \cap (U_x \cap U_y) = A \cap \emptyset = \emptyset. 
    \]
    Thus we have found disjoint open sets in $A$ 
    $A \cap U_x, \ A \cap U_y$ containing 
    $x$ and $y$, respectively. 

    As $x,y$ were arbitrary, the Hausdorff property is satisfied. 
\end{proof}

\begin{prob*}[Munkres 17.13]
    Show that $X$ is Hausdorff if and only if 
    the diagonal $\Delta = \{x \times x \bigm| x \in X\}$ is 
    closed in $X \times X$. 
\end{prob*}

\begin{proof}
    Suppose $X$ is Hausdorff. Then 
    for any $x, y \in X$ we can find 
    disjoint open neighborhoods $U_x, \ U_y$ for them.  
 
    Take $(x,y) \in X\times X \setminus \Delta$. 
    Since $x \neq y$ there are disjoint 
    open sets $U_x, \ U_y$ for the points. 
    Then $U_x \times U_y$ cannot contain 
    a point of the form $(z,z)$. Suppose $(z,z) \in U_x \times U_y$. 
    Then $z \in U_x$ and $z \in U_y$ so $U_x \cap U_y \neq \emptyset$
    which is a contradiction. 
    Thus every point outside $\Delta$ has an open 
    neighborhood disjoint from $\Delta$ so 
    $X\times X \setminus \Delta$ is open and 
    hence $\Delta$ is closed. 

    Now suppose $\Delta$ is closed, meaning 
    $X \times X \setminus \Delta$ is open. 
    Take distinct $x,y \in X$. Then $(x,y) \not\in \Delta$ 
    so there is an open set 
    $U \times V \subseteq X \times X \setminus \Delta$ 
    containing $(x,y)$. 
    
    The claim then is that $U \cap V = \emptyset$. 
    Suppose not. If some $z \in U \cap V$, then 
    $(z,z) \in U \times V$, but $(z,z) \in \Delta$ so 
    we arrive at a contradiction. 
    Since $U,V$ are open neighborhoods of $x$ and $y$, respectively 
    and those points were arbitrary, the space is Hausdorff. 
\end{proof} 


\begin{prob*}[Munkres 20.3]
    Let $X$ be a metric space with metric 
    $d$. 
    \begin{enumerate}[label=\alph*)]
        \item Show that $d : X \times X \to \mathbb R$ is continuous. 
        \item Let $X'$ denote a space having the 
            same underlying set as $X$. Show that 
            if $d : X' \times X' \to \mathbb R$ is continuous, 
            then the topology of $X'$ is finer than 
            the topology of $X$.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{prob*}

\begin{proof}[Proof of (a)]
    The metric $d$ satisfies 
    \begin{align*}
        d(x,y) &\geq 0 \\ 
        d(x,y) = 0 &\Leftrightarrow x = y \\ 
        d(x,y) &= d(y,x) \\ 
        d(x,z) &\leq d(x,y) + d(y,z). 
    \end{align*}
    In particular, 
    \[
        |d(x,y) - d(x',y')| \leq d(x,x') + d(y,y'). 
    \]
    Fix some $(x_0,y_0) \in X\times X$. 
    Let $\varepsilon > 0$ and consider 
    the product neighborhood 
    \[
        B_{\varepsilon/2}(x_0) \times B_{\varepsilon/2}(y_0).
    \]
    If $(x,y) \in B_{\varepsilon/2}(x_0) 
    \times B_{\varepsilon/2}(y_0)$, 
    then 
    \[
        d(x,x_0) < \frac{\varepsilon}{2}, \ 
        d(y,y_0) < \frac{\varepsilon}{2}. 
    \]
    By the triangle-inequality, 
    \[
        d(x,y) \leq d(x,x_0) + d(x_0, y_0) 
        + d(y_0, y), 
    \]
    so 
    \[
        d(x,y) - d(x_0,y_0) 
        \leq d(x,x_0) + d(y,y_0). 
    \]
    Therefore 
    \[
        |d(x,y) - d(x_0,y_0)| 
        \leq d(x,x_0) + d(y,y_0) < \varepsilon. 
    \]
    Hence 
    \[
        d(x,y) \in d(x_0,y_0) \pm \varepsilon 
    \]
    which is open in $\mathbb R$. 
\end{proof}
