\begin{prob*}[2 Problem 5]
    Given $7$ jobs with corresponding 
    processing times, 
    \begin{center}
        \begin{tabular}{ |c|c|c|c|c|c|c|c| } 
            \hline
            \keyterm{Job} $j$ & 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 & 6 & 7 \\ 
            \keyterm{Processing Time} $p_j$ & 4 & 7 & 2 & 9 & 12 & 5 & 3 \\ 
            \hline
        \end{tabular}
    \end{center}

    The jobs will be processed on $3$ identical machines 
    without interruption. All jobs are avliable at 
    time $0$. (Model on slide 13, Chapter 2)
    
    \begin{enumerate}[label=\alph*)]
        \item Use the local search algorithm where we have 
            all jobs on machine $1$ in the beginning (in the 
            same order as they are given). When you move 
            a job, move it to the machine it can start 
            earliest. I there is a tie, use the machine with the lowest number. What is the result, both
            time when the last job finish and the actual schedule?
        \item Use the Greedy algorithm on the same data. When have all jobs finished?
        \item Use the Longest processing time rule. When have all jobs finished?
        \item Give a lower bound on the best solution and give a short explanation?
        \item Is it possible to achieve the answer in d) (Explain)?
    \end{enumerate}
\end{prob*}

This is the classical identical parallel-machine makespan 
minimization problem, denoted 
\[
    P_3 \| \ \| C_{\max}. 
\]
We want to find a schedule minimizing 
\[
    C_{\max} = \max_{i\in[3]} L_i,  
\]
where 
\[
    L_i := \sum_{j \ \text{assigned to machine} \ i} p_j
\]
is the total processing time on machine $i$.


\begin{proof}[Solution to (a)]
    The initial schedule gives all seven jobs to 
    machine $1$ in the described order by 
    \[
        M_1 = \underbrace{1}_{4}
        \qquad 
        \underbrace{1}_{4}
        \qquad 
        \underbrace{2}_{7}
        \qquad 
        \underbrace{3}_{2}
        \qquad 
        \underbrace{4}_{9}
        \qquad 
        \underbrace{5}_{12}
        \qquad 
        \underbrace{6}_{5}
        \qquad 
        \underbrace{7}_{3}.
    \]
    Note: This accidentally looks a lot like fractions, but 
    I am just illustrating the processing times $p_j$. 

    We get initial loads 
    \[
        (L_1,L_2,L_3) = (42,0,0)
    \]
    with $C_{\max} = 42$. 

    If $M_j$ is the unique machine for which 
    $L_j = C_{\max}$ we find that it is advantageous 
    to swap if 
    \[
        L_j + c < L_i, 
    \]
    or equivalently 
    \[
        c < L_i - L_j.
    \]

    We perform the following procedure. 

    \begin{algorithmic}[1]
        \Require Jobs $J$ with processing times $p_j$,
                 machines $M_1,\ldots,M_m$
        \Ensure A locally optimal schedule

        \State Assign all jobs to $M_1$ in their given order
        \State $L_i \gets \sum_{j \in M_i} p_j$
               for each $i \in \{1,\ldots,m\}$

        \While{true}
            \State $i \gets \arg\max_{k} L_k$
            \State $\ell \gets$ last job on $M_i$
            \State $j \gets \arg\min_{k \neq i} L_k$

            \If{$L_j + p_\ell \geq L_i$}
                \State \Return $(M_1,\ldots,M_m)$
            \EndIf

            \State Remove $\ell$ from $M_i$
            \State Append $\ell$ to $M_j$
            \State $L_i \gets L_i - p_\ell$
            \State $L_j \gets L_j + p_\ell$
        \EndWhile
    \end{algorithmic}

    So here we begin with 
    \[
        (42,0,0). 
    \]
    The job which finishes last is $j = 7$ so we get 
    \[
        (42,0,0) \mapsto (39,3,0). 
    \]
    Now $M_1$ still is the machine with maximum load 
    so we take $j = 6$, and move 
    \[
        (39,3,0) \mapsto (34, 3, 5). 
    \]
    Repeat to get 
    \[
        (34,3,5) \mapsto (22,15,5) 
        \mapsto (13,15,14). 
    \]
    Now we look at $M_1$ again and see that 
    for $j = 3$ we have $p_j = 3 < L_3 = 14$ so 
    the algorithm halts with 
    \[
        C_{\max} = 15 \leq 2C^*_{\max} = 
        14 \cdot 2 = 28
    \]
    as expected. 

    Running time is $O(nm)$.

\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Solution to (b)]
    We perform 
    \begin{algorithmic}[1]
        \Require Jobs with processing times
                 $p_1,\ldots,p_n$ and $m$ machines
        \State $L_i \gets 0$ for all $i=1,\ldots,m$
        \For{$j=1,\ldots,n$}
            \State $i^* \gets \arg\min_i L_i$
            \State Assign job $j$ to machine $M_{i^*}$
            \State $L_{i^*} \gets L_{i^*}+p_j$
        \EndFor
        \State \Return $\max_i L_i$
    \end{algorithmic}

    We begin at $j = 1$ and since all $M_i$ have 
    $L_i = 0$ we get $L_i$'s 
    \[
        (4,0,0). 
    \]
    Next 
    \[
        (4,7,0), 
    \]
    then 
    \[
        (4,7,2).
    \]
    Once again $L_3$ is the minimum so next pass gives 
    \[
        (4,7,11). 
    \]
    Now $L_1$ is smallest so we get 
    \[
        (16,7,11). 
    \]
    Now $L_2$ is smallest so we get 
    \[
        (16,12,11). 
    \]
    Now $L_3$ is smallest so we get 
    \[
        (16,12,14). 
    \]
    We obtain $C_{\max} = 16$ with 
    the algorithm running in time $O(nm)$. 
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Solution to (c)]
    We perform 
    \begin{algorithmic}[1]
        \Require Jobs $J$ with processing times $p_j$,
                 $m$ identical machines
        \State Sort jobs by nonincreasing processing time
        \State $L_i \gets 0$ for all $i=1,\ldots,m$
        \For{each job $j$ in sorted order}
            \State $i^* \gets \arg\min_i L_i$
            \State Assign job $j$ to machine $M_{i^*}$
            \State $L_{i^*} \gets L_{i^*}+p_j$
        \EndFor
        \State \Return $\max_i L_i$
    \end{algorithmic}

    Performiing the algorithm we get 
    \begin{align*}
        (12,0,0) &\mapsto (12,9,0) \\ 
        &\mapsto (12,9,7) \\ 
        &\mapsto (12,9,12) \\ 
        &\mapsto (12,13,12) \\ 
        &\mapsto (15,13,12) \\ 
        &\mapsto (15,13,12)
    \end{align*}
    attaining a maximum of $15$. 

    Running time is 
    $O(n\log n + mn)$ since we sort first. 
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Solutioin and proof sketch for (d)]
    Let $C_{\max}^*$ denote the optimal makespan, and 
    let $L_i$ be the total processing time assigned 
    to machine $M_i$. 

    Since every job must be processed, we have 
    \[
        \sum_{i=1}^m L_i = \sum_{j=1}^n p_j. 
    \]
    Moreover, since each machine processes jobs 
    sequentially, 
    \[
        C_{\max}^* \geq \max_i L_i \geq \frac 1 m 
        \sum_{i=1}^m L_i = \frac 1 m \sum_{j=1}^n p_j. 
    \]
    We also necessarily have 
    \[
        C_{\max}^* \geq \max_j p_j, 
    \]
    since every job must finish before the 
    schedule is complete. 

    Combining we get 
    \[
        C_{\max}^* \geq 
        \max \left\{
            \frac 1 m \sum_{j=1}^n p_j, \ \max_j p_j
        \right\}.
    \]
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Solution to (e)]
    Consider the following schedule, 
    \begin{align*}
        M_1 : \{5,3\},  & L_1 = 12 + 2 = 14 \\ 
        M_2 : \{4,6\},  & L_2 = 9+5 = 14, \\ 
        M_3 : \{2,1,7\} & L_3 = 7+4+3 = 14.
    \end{align*}

    This is a feasible schedule with 
    \[
        C_{\max} = \max\{14,14,14\} = 14 = C_{\max}^*. 
    \]
\end{proof}

\begin{prob*}[2, Problem 6]
    The adjacency matrix of a graph on $6$ vertices 
    is given below. The triangle inequality holds for the 
    whole graph. 
    \[
        \begin{bmatrix}
            0 & 4 & 5 &10&11& 7 \\ 
            4 & 0 & 9 & 6& 7& 9 \\ 
            5 & 9 & 0 & 9& 6& 2 \\ 
           10 & 6 & 9 & 0& 3& 9 \\ 
           11 & 7 & 6 & 3& 0& 6 \\ 
            7 & 9 & 2 & 9& 6& 0 
        \end{bmatrix}
    \]
    \begin{enumerate}[label=\alph*)]
        \item Verify that the triangle inquality 
            holds when going from vertex $1$ to $5$. 
        \item Run the nearest addition algorithm on 
            the graph. If there are more than 
            one candidate to add in a step, use the vertex 
            with the smallest number. It is sufficient to 
            have fours nodes in the tour (skipping the 
            last two iterations through the loop).
        \item Run the Double tree algorithm on the graph. 
            If there are more than one minimum spanning 
            tree, you should run it on every tree. 
        \item Run Christofides' algorithm on the graph. 
            If there are more than one minimum spanning 
            tree, you should run it on every tree. 
    \end{enumerate}
\end{prob*}

Let $G = ([6], E) \cong K_6$ be the complete 
graph (undirected) on $6$ vertices with edge weights given 
in the adjacency matrix in the problem description.

Denote the weights $c_{ij}$. I.e. 
$c_{12} = 4, \ c_{15} = 11$ and so forth. 

\begin{proof}[Solution for (a)]
    We have $c_{15} = 11$. 
    The possibilities are 
    \[
        \begin{aligned}
        c_{12}+c_{25}&=4+7=11,\\
        c_{13}+c_{35}&=5+6=11,\\
        c_{14}+c_{45}&=10+3=13,\\
        c_{16}+c_{65}&=7+6=13.
        \end{aligned}
    \]
    Thus 
    \[
        c_{15} = 11 \leq \min\{11,11,13,13\} = 11 
    \]
    so we're good.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Solution to (b)]
    We wish to find a permutation $\pi$ of the 
    vertices minimizing 
    \[
        c_{\pi(1)\pi(2)} + 
        c_{\pi(2)\pi(3)} + \cdots + 
        c_{\pi(6)\pi(1)}. 
    \]

    I.e. we want to find 
    a minimum-weight Hamiltonian cycle. 
    The additional assumption is that the weights 
    satisfy the triangle inequality 
    \[
        c_{ij} \leq c_{ik} + c_{ki}, \ \forall i,j,k \in V = [6].
    \]

    We begin with the two closest vertices 
    and repeatedly add the vertex outside 
    the tour that is closest to any vertex 
    already in the tour. 

    When inserting a new vertex $j$ between 
    two consecutive vertices $u,v$, the increase in 
    tour cost is 
    \[
        \Delta = c_{uj} + c_{jv} - c_{uv}.
    \]

    \textbf{Iteration 0: Init.}

    The smallest edge weight is 
    \[
        c_{36} = 2. 
    \]
    Thus, the initial tour is 
    \[
        T_0 = (3,6,3), 
    \]
    with cost $4$. 

    \textbf{Iteration 1.}

    For each unvisited vertex $v$, compute 
    its distance to $S = \{3,6\}$, 
    \begin{align*}
        d(1,S) &= 5 \\ 
        d(2,S) &= 9 \\ 
        d(4,S) &= 9 \\ 
        d(5,S) &= 6. 
    \end{align*}
    Therefore, we choose vertex $1$, whose nearest 
    vertex is $3$. 
    We insert it into the tour to get 
    \[
        T_1 = (3,1,6,3). 
    \] 
    The cost is $w(T_1) = 14$. 

    \textbf{Iteration 2.}

    Repeat to find $\underset{v}{\arg\min} d(v,S) = 2$ with 
    weight $4$. 
    We have to places we can set it. 
    If we insert it between $3$ and $1$ we get 
    \[
        \Delta_1 = c_{32} + c_{21} - c_{31} 
        = 8. 
    \]
    If we choose $1$ and $6$ we get 
    \[
        \Delta_2 = 6. 
    \]
    Thus we choose it such that 
    \[
        T_2 = (3,1,2,6,3).
    \]
    Now the cost is $w(T_2) = 20$. 
    For the grader, 
    \[
        \text{weight is} \ \boxed{20}
    \]
\end{proof}
\begin{proof}[Solution to (c)]
    The four smallest edge weights are 
    \begin{align*}
        c_{36} &= 2, \\
        c_{45} &= 3, \\
        c_{12} &= 4, \\
        c_{13} &= 5. 
    \end{align*}
    Kruskal's algorithm selects all four, giving 
    two connected components in 
    \[
        A = \{1,2,3,6\}, \ B = \{4,5\}.
    \]

    The minimum weight of an edge joining 
    these components is $6$, attained by the 
    edges 
    \[
        \{2,4\}, \ \{3,5\}, \ \{5,6\}. 
    \]
    Formally, defininig 
    \[
        F = \{\{3,6\}, \ 
              \{4,5\}, \ 
              \{1,2\}, \ 
              \{1,3\}\},  
    \]
    the three MSTs are 
    \begin{align*}
        T_A &= F \cup \{\{2,4\}\} \\ 
        T_B &= F \cup \{\{3,5\}\} \\ 
        T_C &= F \cup \{\{5,6\}\}. 
    \end{align*}
    Each has total weight 
    \[
        w(T_i) = 20. 
    \]

    Every edge in each MST is doubled, making every 
    vertex degree even. Hence each resulting multigraph 
    admits an Eulerian circuit. 

    We start at vertex $1$ in each case, traversing 
    tree branches in increasing order of vertex number. 

    For $T_A$, an Eulerian circuit is 
    \[
        W_A = (1,2,4,5,4,2,1,3,6,3,1). 
    \]
    Shortcutting repeated vertices yields 
    \[
        H_A = (1,2,4,5,3,6,1). 
    \]
    Its cost is 
    \[
        w(H_A) = 4+6+3+6+2+7 = 28. 
    \]
    For $T_B$ take 
    \[
        W_B = (1,2,1,3,5,4,5,3,6,3,1). 
    \]
    Shortcutting gives 
    \[
        H_B=(1,2,3,5,4,6,1),
    \]
    with cost 
    \[
        w(H_B) = 38. 
    \]
    Finally, for $T_C$, take 
    \[
        W_C=(1,2,1,3,6,5,4,5,6,3,1).
    \]
    As before, 
    \[
        H_C=(1,2,3,6,5,4,1),
    \]
    with 
    \[
        w(H_C) = 34. 
    \]

    All three satisfy 
    \[
        w(H_i) \leq 2w(T_i) = 40, 
    \]
    very cool. 
\end{proof}

Recall that we want to maaximize 
\[
    \sum_{i=1}^6y v_ix_i 
\]
such that 
\[
    \sum_{i=1}^6 s_ix_i \leq 10, 
    \ x_i \in \{0,1\}. 
\]

We define $A(i) \subseteq \mathbb N_0 \times 
\mathbb N_0$ where each pair 
$(s,v) \in A(i)$ represents a feasible choice 
of items from $[i]$ having total size $s$ and 
value $v$. 

We denote 
\[
    A'(i) = A(i-1) \cup \{(s + s_i, v + v_i) 
    \bigm| (s,v) \in A(i-1), \ s+s_i \leq B\}. 
\]

    leaving 
    \[

    \textbf{Iteration 6.}

    We generate

    Here

    We have

    independently assigning each variable the value
    $\mathrm{True}$ with probability $1/2$.

import random

def randomized_max_sat(clauses):
    variables = {
        abs(lit)
        for weight, clause in clauses
        for lit in clause
    }

\begin{proof}[Solution to (b)]

               +\frac34(4+3+2+2+5)\\

    \[
        E_b^{(i)}
        =\mathbb E[Z\mid
            x_1=a_1,\ldots,x_{i-1}=a_{i-1},x_i=b]
    \]
    for $b\in\{0,1\}$. We choose
    \[
        a_i\in\arg\max_{b\in\{0,1\}}E_b^{(i)}.

    For $x_1$, only clauses $C_1,\ldots,C_4$

        \end{aligned}

    \]

\begin{proof}[Statement and proof sketch of (a/b)]

    Intuitively,  
    we'd want something like: 
    
    Maximize 
    \[
        \sum_{i=1}^4 w_i\left(\bigoplus_{\ell \in C_i} \ell\right), 
    \]
    subject to 
    \[
        x_1,x_2,x_3,x_4 \in \{0,1\},  
    \]
    but this isn't a valid ILP, of course. 

    So instead let $x_1,x_2,x_3,x_4 \in \{0,1\}$ 
    and introduce $y_1,y_2,y_3,y_4 \in \{0,1\}$ 
    where $y_i = 1$ indicates that $C_i$ is satisfied. 

    We maximize 
    \[
        4y_1 + 3y_2 + 2y_3 + y_4, 
    \]
    subject to 
    \[
        \begin{aligned}
            x_1 + (1 - x_3) &\geq y_1, \\ 
            (1-x_1) + x_4   &\geq y_2, \\ 
            (1-x_2) + x_4   &\geq y_3, \\ 
        (1-x_2) + (1 - x_3) &\geq y_4.  
        \end{aligned}
    \]

    This is a valid ILP formulation and we claim 
    that it corresponds one-to-one with the MAX SAT instance. 

    \textbf{$\operatorname{OPT}_{\text{ILP}} 
    \geq \operatorname{OPT}_{\text{MAXSAT}}$.}

    Let $x^*$ be an optimal truth assignment 
    for the MAX-SAT instance. 
    
    Construct an ILP solution by choosing 
    the same values for the variables $x_j$ and 
    setting 
    \[
        y_i = 
        \begin{cases}
            1 & \text{if} \ C_i \ \text{is satisfied by} \ x^* \\ 
            0 & \text{otherwise}. 
        \end{cases}
    \]
    We claim this solution is feasible. 

    Indeed, if $C_i$ is satisfied, at least on 
    literal evaluates to $1$, and therefore 
    \[
        \sum_{i\in C_i}v(\ell) \geq 1 = y_i, 
    \]
    where we denote 
    \[
        v(\ell) 
        = \begin{cases}
            x_j & \text{if} \ \ell = x_j \\ 
            1 - x_j & \text{if} \ \ell = \bar x_j. 
        \end{cases}
    \]

    Otherwise, $y_i = 0$ and the constraint holds 
    because every literal evaluates to a nonnegative 
    number. 

    Thus, the constructed solution is feasible, and 
    its objective value is the total weight of clauses 
    satisfied by $x^*$. 

    Consequently 
    \[
        \operatorname{OPT}_{\text{ILP}} 
    \geq \operatorname{OPT}_{\text{MAXSAT}}. 
    \]

    \textbf{$\operatorname{OPT}_{\text{ILP}} 
    \leq \operatorname{OPT}_{\text{MAXSAT}}$.}

    Now let $(x^*, y^*)$ be an optimal ILP 
    solution. 

    Consider the truth assignment obtained by 
    interpreting $x_j^* = 1$ as true and 
    $0$ as false. 

    For every clause $C_i$ with $y^*_i = 1$, 
    the corresponding constraint gives 
    \[
        \sum_{\ell \in C_i} v(\ell) \geq 1. 
    \]

    Sinc eterms are binary, at least 
    one literal must evaluate to $1$. Hence $C_i$ is 
    satisfied. 

    Therefore, the assignment satisfies at 
    least all clauses for which $y^* = 1$. 
    Since weights are positive, its total weight is 
    at least 
    \[
        \sum_{i=1}^4 w_iy^*_i = \operatorname{OPT}_{\text{ILP}}.
    \]
    But the optimal MAX-SAT assignment cannot have 
    a smaller value than any particular assignment. Thus 
    \[
        \operatorname{OPT}_{\text{ILp}} 
        \leq 
        \operatorname{OPT}_{\text{MAXSAT}}. 
    \]  

    For the LP relaxation we simply replace the constraints 
    by contiuous bounds 
    \[
        x_i \in [0,1], \ y_i \in [0,1]. 
    \]

    \textbf{Asside.}

    Also, for fun, here is MAX-SAT encoded as a 
    maximisation problem in a  
    a quotient ring, because that's funny. 

    For the polynomial ring 
    \[
        \mathbb Q[x_1,x_2,x_3,x_4] \qquad (\text{nice 
        and computable}),  
    \]
    notice that 
    \[
        x_i \in \{0,1\} \Leftrightarrow 
        x_i^2 - x_i = 0. 
    \]
    Define the ideal 
    \[
        I = (x^2_1 - x_1, \ldots, x_4^2 - x_4) 
    \]
    and quotient to get 
    \[
        R = \mathbb Q[x_1,x_2,x_3,x_4]/I. 
    \]
    The affine variety $V(I)$ is 
    \[
        V(I) = \{0,1\}^4. 
    \]
    By the Chinese remainder theorem, 
    \[
        R \cong \prod_{a \in \{0,1\}} 
        \mathbb Q \cong \mathbb Q^{16}. 
    \]

    We want a polynomial $s_i$ evaluating to $1$ 
    when $C_i$ is satisfied. 

    For our clauses the following  
    \[
        \begin{aligned}
          s_1&=1-(1-x_1)x_3,\\
          s_2&=1-x_1(1-x_4),\\
          s_3&=1-x_2(1-x_4),\\
          s_4&=1-x_2x_3(1-x_4)
        \end{aligned}
    \]
    work. 

    For each $s_i$ we have 
    \[
        s_i(a)=
        \begin{cases}
        1&\text{if }a\models C_i,\\
        0&\text{otherwise}.
        \end{cases}
    \]
    Setting $F = 4s_1 + 3s_2 + 2s_3 + s_4$ gives 
    our objective function and we can reformulate the 
    problem as 
    \[
        \max_{a \in V(I)} F(a).
    \]

    Since all weights are integers we have 
    \[
        F(a) \in \{0,1,\ldots,10\}. 
    \]
    So 
    \[
        F(a) \geq k 
    \]
    iff 
    \[
        \prod_{r = k}^{10} (F(a) - r) = 0, 
    \]
    letting us define an ideal 
    \[
        J_k = I + \left( \prod_{r=k}(F-r)\right), 
    \]
    giving way to 
    \[
        \operatorname{OPT}_{\mathrm{MAXSAT}}\ge k
        \iff
        V(J_k)\ne\varnothing
        \iff
        S/J_k\ne 0.
    \]

    If wee consider the 
    $\mathbb Q$-subalgebra generated by $F$, 
    \[
        \mathbb Q[F] \subseteq R, 
    \] 
    we can, by the first isomorphism theorem, say 
    that the evaluation homomorphism 
    \[
        \varphi : \mathbb Q[t] \to R, \ t \mapsto F, 
    \]
    gives 
    \[
        \mathbb Q[F] \cong \mathbb Q[t]/\ker \varphi. 
    \]
    This gives 
    \[
        \ker \varphi 
        = \left(\prod_{9 \neq i=3}^{10} (t - i)\right),
    \]
    so 
    \[
        \mathbb Q[F] = 
        \frac{\mathbb Q[t]}{\prod_{i \neq i=3}^{10} (t-i)}.
    \]
    Then the largest root is the optimal MAX-SAT value. 

    We have $R \cong \mathbb Q^{2^n}$ so this would 
    probably be ridiculously inefficient for 
    large MAX-SAT instances, but its way 
    cooler than integer programming.     
\end{proof}

\begin{prob*}[5, Problem 1]
    The graph below is $3$-colorable. Use 
    the algorithm that colors a $3$-colorable graph 
    with $O(\sqrt n)$ colors, 
    Chapter $6$ to color the graph. 
    Every time you have a choice between 
    nodes, choose the lowered number one. 
    Notice, it is important to understand 
    the algorithm, not find the best coloring. 
\end{prob*}
\begin{proof}[Solution]
    I believe we are meant to use Avi Widgerson's 
    algorithm as it is a 
    famous (promised-$3$-colorable) 
    coloring algorithm giving $O(\sqrt n)$ colors. 

    We remind ourselves how the algorithm goes. 

    Let $n = |V|$ throughout the algorithm. 
    While $\Delta(G) > \sqrt n$, choose a vertex $v$ 
    of maximum degree. Color its induced neighborhood 
    $G[N(v)]$ using two colors, give $v$ a 
    third color, and remove $N[v]$. 

    Once $\Delta(G) \leq \sqrt n$, greedily color 
    the remaining graph. 

    Simple enough! 

    At iteration $1$ we have 
    $\Delta(G) = 4 > 3 = \sqrt n$. 
    Both $1$ and $6$ have maximum degree. By the 
    tie-breaking rule we choose the first. 

    The neighborhood is 
    \[
        N(1) = \{3,4,6,8\}. 
    \]
    The induced subgraph is the path 
    $4$--$3$--$6$--$8$. 

    We $2$-color by 
    \[
        \begin{aligned}
        \text{Color 1}:&\quad \{3,8\},\\
        \text{Color 2}:&\quad \{4,6\}.
        \end{aligned}
    \]

    Give vertex $1$ color $3$. 

    Remove 
    \[
        N[1]=\{1,3,4,6,8\}
    \]
    to get 
    \[
        V'=\{2,5,7,9\}.
    \]


    The remaining edges are 
    \[
        E'=\{\{2,7\},\{2,9\},\{5,9\},\{7,9\}\}.
    \]

    Now $\Delta(G)$ is small enough that we 
    go to phase $2$. 

    We now greedily color in numerical order. 
    Using Wigderson's formulation, the 
    next avaliable color is color $3$. 

    Then 
    \[
        \begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|}
            \keyterm{Vertex} & 
            \keyterm{Previosuly colored neighbors 
            in $G'$} & \keyterm{Assigned color} \\ 
            2 & None & 3 \\ 
            5 & None & 3 \\ 
            7 & 2 (color 3) & 4 \\ 
            9 & 2,5,7 (colors 3,3,4) & 5.
        \end{tabular}
    \]
    Thus the final coloring is 
    \[
        \begin{aligned}
        V_1&=\{3,8\},\\
        V_2&=\{4,6\},\\
        V_3&=\{1,2,5\},\\
        V_4&=\{7\},\\
        V_5&=\{9\}.
        \end{aligned}
    \]

    If we don't employ Wigderson's formulation 
    allowing color $3$ to be valid, since $1$ had 
    no neighbors among the remaining vertices, 
    then we might end up with $7$ colors instead. 

    A cool observation is that, while 
    this algorithm runs in $O((n+m)\sqrt n)$ (where 
    $m = |E|$, also, running time can be improved) time 
    with a guarantee of $O(\sqrt n)$ colors, 
    it was conjectured that $k$-colorability of 
    $3$-colorable graphs is NP-hard for all $k$, 
    only that it was really hard to prove. 

    This algorithm gives some insight as to why, since 
    for $k = O(\sqrt n)$ we (given all reasonable assumptions)
    cannot have hardness, making 
    the difficulty of proving the difficulty 
    harder for increasing $k$. 
    
    However, as Fei, Minzer, and Wang proved 
    the $4$-to-$1$ Games Conjecture back in september, 
    we have that $k$-coloring of promised-$3$-colorable 
    graphs is NP-hard $\forall k \geq 3$.  

    Very cool!
    
\end{proof}
