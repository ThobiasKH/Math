\begin{prob}
    Let 
    \[
        R = \frac{k[x,y]}{(xy)}
    \]
    and write $\overline x, \overline y$ for the residue classes. 

    What is $(\overline x)$ as an ideal of $R$? 

    Compute 
    \[
        R/(\bar x),\qquad R/(\bar y),\qquad R/(\bar x,\bar y).
    \]
    Then classify them as prime/maximal/neither. 
\end{prob}
\begin{proof}[Sol]
    We note that the ideal 
    generated by $\overline x = x + (xy)$ is 
    \[
        (\overline x) = 
        \{\overline{xf} \bigm| f \in k[x,y]\}. 
    \]
    We have $(xy) \subseteq (x)$ and 
    $(xy) \subseteq (y)$ as ideals of $k[x,y]$. 

    Consider the projection $\pi : k[x,y] \to R$ 
    by 
    \[
        \pi(a) = \overline a = a + (xy). 
    \]
    Since $\pi$ is surjective and $\{x\} \subseteq k[x,y]$ we have 
    \[
        \pi[(x)] = (\pi x) = (\overline x),  
    \]
    (note: surjectivity guarantees that scalar multiplication in 
    $R$ can be pulled back to $k[x,y]$, ensuring 
    $\pi[(x)] \supseteq (\pi(x))$)
    but also 
    \[
        \pi[(x)] = \frac{(x)}{(xy)}
    \]
    since $(xy) \subseteq (x)$. 

    Similarly,
    \[
        \frac{(y)}{(xy)} = \pi[(y)] = (\overline y),
    \]
    and 
    \[
        \frac{(x,y)}{(xy)} = \pi[(x,y)] = (\overline x, \overline y).
    \]

    Thus we can apply the third isomorphism theorem to get 
    \[
        \frac{R}{(\overline x)} = \frac{k[x,y]/(xy)}{(x)/(xy)} 
        \cong \frac{k[x,y]}{(x)} \cong k[y], 
    \]
    and 
    \[
        \frac{R}{(\overline y)} = \frac{k[x,y]/(xy)}{(y)/(xy)} 
        \cong \frac{k[x,y]}{(y)} \cong k[x], 
    \]
    and 
    \[
        \frac{R}{(\overline x, \overline y)} = 
        \frac{k[x,y]/(xy)}{(x, y)/(xy)} 
        \cong \frac{k[x,y]}{(x, y)} \cong k.  
    \]

    The first two are integral domains so 
    $(\overline x), (\overline y)$ are prime, though not 
    maximal, while 
    $(\overline x, \overline y)$ is maximal since 
    $R/(\overline x, \overline y) \cong k$ is a field. 
\end{proof}

\begin{prob}
    Let 
    \[
        R = \frac{k[x,y]}{(xy)}, 
    \]
    and let 
    \[
        \pi : k[x,y] \to R
    \]
    be the quotient map. 

    Prove that every prime ideal $P \subseteq R$ 
    contains $\overline x$ or $\overline y$. 

    Then, classify every prime ideal of $R$. 

    You may use without proof: 
    \[
        P \subseteq R \ \text{prime} 
        \Leftrightarrow \pi^{-1}(P) \subseteq k[x,y] 
        \ \text{is prime containing} \ (xy). 
    \]
\end{prob}

\begin{proof}
    We have $\overline x \overline y = 0$, and since 
    $P$ is an ideal, and thus a group, we have 
    $0 \in P$, and since $P$ is prime we must then have 
    \[\overline x \in P \ \text{or} \ \overline y \in P.\]

    Suppose $P$ contains $\overline x$. 
    Then, $(\overline x) \subseteq P$. 

    Then, by the third isomorphism theorem,  
    \[\frac{R/(\overline x)}{P / (\overline x)}         \cong R/P,\]
    but 
    \[\frac{R}{(\overline x)} \cong k[y]\]
    so 
    \[R/P \cong \frac{k[y]}{P/(\overline x)}.\]

    Since $P$ is a prime ideal of $R$, the quotient $R/P$ is an integral domain. Therefore, $P/(\overline x)$ must be a prime ideal of $k[y]$. 

    Since $k[y]$ is a Principal Ideal Domain (PID), its prime ideals are:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item The zero ideal $(0)$, or
        \item Irreducible polynomial ideals $(f(y))$, where $f(y) \in k[y]$ is an irreducible polynomial.
    \end{enumerate}

    Under the canonical quotient map $R \to R/(\overline x) \cong k[y]$, these correspond precisely to the prime ideals of $R$ containing $\overline x$:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $P = (\overline x)$, corresponding to $(0) \subset k[y]$.
        \item $P = (\overline x, f(\overline y))$, where $f(y) \in k[y]$ is irreducible.
    \end{enumerate}

    By symmetric reasoning, if $\overline y \in P$, then $P/(\overline y)$ is a prime ideal of $k[x]$, which yields:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $P = (\overline y)$, corresponding to $(0) \subset k[x]$.
        \item $P = (\overline y, g(\overline x))$, where $g(x) \in k[x]$ is irreducible.
    \end{enumerate}

    Combining both cases, the prime ideals of $R$ consist of:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Minimal prime ideals: $(\overline x)$ and $(\overline y)$.
        \item Maximal ideals: $(\overline x, f(\overline y))$ for irreducible $f(y) \in k[y]$, and $(\overline y, g(\overline x))$ for irreducible $g(x) \in k[x]$.
    \end{itemize}
\end{proof}

\begin{prob}
    Let $R$ be a commutative ring, $I$ an ideal of 
    $R$, and $M$ an $R$-module. 

    You have three objects 
    \[
        IM, \ M, \ M/IM. 
    \]

    \textbf{Part A.}

    Identify the two most natural $R$-linear maps 
    between these objects and use them to construct 
    a short exact sequence. 

    Give the maps explicitly and check exactness 
    at every nonzero term. 

    \textbf{Part B.}

    Now consider $R/I$ as an $R$-module. 

    There is a canonical isomorphism 
    \[
        M \otimes_R R/I \cong M/IM. 
    \]

    Answer: 
    \begin{itemize}
        \item What should the natural map 
            \[
                M \times R/I \to M/IM 
            \]
            probably be? 
        \item Why is there an immediate well-definedness issue? 
        \item What property would allow that bilinear map 
            to induce a map from 
            \[
                M \otimes_R R/I? 
            \]
    \end{itemize}

    \textbf{Part C.}

    Suppose $N \subseteq M$. 
    There is an obvious short exact sequence 
    \[
        0 \to N \to M \to M/N \to 0. 
    \]

    Tensor all with $R/I$. 

    You might hope to get 
    \[
        0 \to N \otimes_R R/I \to M \otimes_R R/I 
        \to (M/N) \otimes_R R/I \to 0. 
    \]

    Is that always exact? 
\end{prob}

\begin{proof}[Proof of A]
    $IM$ is the collection of finite sums of 
    $i_jm_j$ where $i_j \in I$ and $m_j \in M$. 

    $M/IM$ is then the collection of 
    \[
        \overline x = x + IM, \ x \in M, 
    \]
    meaning the residue classes of elements in $M$ modulo 
    $IM$. 

    The most natural map from $M$ to $M/IM$ is the 
    projection $\pi : M \to M/IM$, 
    \[
        \pi(x) = \overline x 
    \]
    sending elements $x$ into their residue classes. 
    
    The natural map from $IM$ to $M$ is the inclusion 
    $\iota : IM \to M$ simply sending 
    \[
        \iota(x) = x
    \]
    since $IM \subseteq M$. 

    $R$-linearity of $\iota$ is essentially 
    trivial since 
    \[
        \iota(rx+y) = rx + y = r\iota(x) + \iota(y). 
    \]

    For $\pi$ observe that 
    \begin{align*}
        \pi(rx + y) 
        &= \overline{rx + y} \\ 
        &= (rx + y) + IM \\ 
        &= (rx + IM) + y + IM \\ 
        &= r(x+IM) + (y + IM) \\ 
        &= r\overline{x} + \overline y \\ 
        &= r\pi(x) + \pi(y).   
    \end{align*}

    This gives a natural sequence  
    \[
        0 \to IM \overset\iota\to M \overset\pi\to M/IM \to 0. 
    \]

    Then 
    \[
        \ker \pi = IM 
    \]
    since elements in $IM$ are $0$ modulo $IM$ 
    when you take the quotient, and 
    $IM \subseteq M$.  

    Then 
    \[
        \im \iota = IM,          
    \]   
    since $\iota$ maps $x$ to $x$ with $IM \subseteq M$. 
    
    We have $\im \pi = M/IM$ since 
    a residue class $\overline x \in M/IM$ has 
    at least one representative $x\in M$. 

    The only map from $M/IM \to 0$ is the trivial map, 
    so 
    \[
        \ker(M/IM \to 0) = M/IM. 
    \]

    For the other side of the sequence there is once 
    again only the trivial map from 
    $0 \to IM$ with $\im(0 \to IM) = 0$, and 
    \[
        \ker \iota = 0,  
    \] 
    since $\iota(x) = 0_M$ implies $x = 0_M$, but 
    as $IM \subseteq M$ we have that $0_{IM} = 0_M$. 

    Thus we have shown exactness 
    at each point in the sequence, so this 
    short sequence is exact.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of B]
    A natural map $\beta : M \times R/I \to M/IM$ 
    might be 
    \[
        (m, \overline x) \overset\beta\mapsto 
        \overline{mx}, 
    \]
    meaning 
    \[
        (m, r + I) \mapsto (mr) + IM 
    \]

    Notice that $(m,\overline x) \mapsto \overline{m + x}$ 
    would very obviously create issues if 
    we try to apply it to the tensor product since 
    we require that $m \otimes 0$ is $0$. 
    Furthermore, $m + r$ is generally not an element 
    of $M$ for arbitrary $r \in R$. 

    For well-definedness suppose 
    $r + I = s + I$. 
    Then $r-s \in I$. 

    Then $(r-s)m \in IM$ since 
    $r-s \in I$ and $m \in M$. 

    Thus we have that 
    \begin{align*}
        \beta(m, r) - \beta(m, s) 
        &= (mr + IM) - (ms + IM) \\ 
        &= (m(r-s)) + IM, 
    \end{align*}  
    bit $m(r-s) \in IM$ so this is $0_{IM}$. 

    Lastly 
    \begin{align*}
        \beta(m_1 + m_2, \overline r) 
        &= ((m_1 + m_2)r) + IM \\ 
        &= (m_1r + m_2r) + IM \\ 
        &= (m_1r + IM) + (m_2r + IM) \\ 
        &= \beta(m_1, \overline r) + \beta(m_2, \overline r),  
    \end{align*}
    and 
    \begin{align*}
        \beta(m, \overline r + \overline s) 
        &= (m(r + s)) + IM \\ 
        &= (mr + ms) + IM \\
        &= (mr + IM) + (ms + IM) \\  
        &= \beta(m, \overline r) + \beta(m, \overline s), 
    \end{align*}
    and 
    \begin{align*}
        \beta(am, \overline r) 
        &= ((am)r) + IM \\ 
        &= (m(ar)) + IM \\ 
        &= \beta(m, a\overline r).  
    \end{align*}

    Thus, by the universal property of 
    the tensor product, we 
    can factor through a unique map so that 
    \[
        \begin{tikzcd}
            M \times R/I \ar[dr] 
            \ar[r, "\beta'"] & M/IM \\ 
                & M \otimes_R R/I \ar[u,dashed, "\exists!"]
        \end{tikzcd}
    \]
    commutes. 
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of C]
    We begin with the short exact sequence 
    \[
        0 \to N \overset\iota\to M 
        \overset\pi\to M/N \to 0. 
    \]

    Tensoring with $R/I$ induces maps 
    $\iota_R : N \otimes_R R/I \to M \otimes_R R/I$ 
    sending
    \[
        n \otimes \overline r
        \overset{\iota_R}\mapsto n \otimes \overline r  
    \]
    by 
    \[
        \iota_R(n\otimes \overline r) 
        = \iota(n) \otimes r, 
    \]
    and
    $\pi_R : M \otimes_R R/I \to (M/N) \otimes_R R/I$ sending
    \[
        m \otimes \overline r \overset{\pi_R}\mapsto 
        \overline m \otimes \overline r 
    \]
    by 
    \[
        \pi_R(m\otimes \overline r) 
        = \pi(m) \otimes \overline r 
        = (m+N) \otimes \overline r. 
    \]

    In general tensoring is right exact, meaning 
    \[
        M' \otimes L 
        \to M'' \otimes L 
        \to M''' \otimes L \to 0 
    \]
    would remaing exact if 
    \[
        M' \to M'' \to M''' \to 0 
    \]
    was. 
    
    Take for instance
    \[
        0 \to 2\mathbb Z \to \mathbb Z 
        \to \mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z \to 0
    \]
    and tensor with $\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z$. 

    Then we imagine we get an exact sequence  
    \[
        0 \to 2 \mathbb Z \otimes_{\mathbb Z} 
        \mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z 
        \to \mathbb Z \otimes_{\mathbb Z} \mathbb Z / 
        2 \mathbb Z \to \mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z 
        \otimes_{\mathbb Z} \mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z
        \to 0. 
    \]
    However, there is no such exact sequence, 
    with the 
    \[
        0 \to 2\mathbb Z \otimes_{\mathbb Z} 
        \mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z
    \]
    term being the problem.

    Consider $2\mathbb Z \otimes_{\mathbb Z} 
    \mathbb Z/2 \to \mathbb Z \otimes_{\mathbb Z} 
    \mathbb Z/2$. 
    Take $2 \otimes \overline 1$. Then the image 
    is $2 \otimes \overline 1$, but 
    in $\mathbb Z \otimes \mathbb Z/2$ we have 
    \[
        (2\cdot 1) \otimes \overline 1 
        = 1 \otimes(2\overline 1) = 0. 
    \]

    In $2\mathbb Z \otimes \mathbb Z/2$, however, 
    $2 \otimes \overline 1$ is nonzero. 
    Take the $\mathbb Z$-bilinear map 
    $B : 2\mathbb Z \times \mathbb Z/2 \to \mathbb Z_2$ 
    given by $B(2m, \overline n) = mn \ (\mod 2)$ and 
    take the unique map factoring through 
    the tensor product $\beta : 2\mathbb Z \otimes 
    \mathbb Z/2 \to \mathbb Z_2$. 

    As an asside, the map $B$ is well defined 
    since taking the product of two 
    integers modulo $2$ gives an element in 
    $\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z \cong \mathbb Z_2$. 
    
    It is linear since 
    \begin{align*}
        B(2m + 2m', n) 
        &= (2m + 2m')n \mod 2 \\ 
        &= 2mn + 2m'n \mod 2 \\ 
        &= B(2m, n) + B(2m'n) 
    \end{align*}    
    in $\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z$. 
    The argument for linearity in the second 
    coordinate and for scalar multipliaction 
    from $\mathbb Z$ on the coordinates 
    is analogous.
    
    Then 
    \[
        \beta(2\otimes 1) = 1 \neq 0. 
    \]

    Thus there is a nonzero element sent to $0$, so 
    \[
        \ker(\iota_R) \neq 0. 
    \]


\end{proof}

\begin{prob}
    Let $M$ be an $R$-module and let 
    $A,B \leq M$ be submodules. 
    You are given 
    \[
        A \cap B, \ A, \ B, \ A + B. 
    \]
    Construct a natural short exact 
    sequence involving all. 
\end{prob}
\begin{proof}[Sol.]
    We note that 
    \[
        A + B \supseteq A \supseteq A \cap B 
    \]
    and similarly, 
    \[
        A + B \supseteq B \supseteq A \cap B. 
    \]

    Thus there are very natural inclusion maps 
    \[
        A \cap B \to A, \ A \cap B \to B, \ 
        A \to A + B, \ B \to A + B.  
    \] 

    However, these are not what we are looking for exactly. 

    The observation that 
    \[
        \frac{A+B}{B} \cong \frac{A}{A \cap B }
    \]
    gives us what we need. 

    Let $\iota: A \cap B \to A$ be the inclusion, which is 
    well-defined since $A \cap B \subseteq A$.  

    Then define $\pi : A \to (A+B)/B$ to be the map 
    \[
        \pi(a) = a + B. 
    \]
    This feels like the projection map since for $a+b$ in 
    $A+B$ we have $(a+b) + B = a + B$ since $b \in B$. 

    It is not terribly hard to see that these are 
    homomorphisms. 

    Then 
    \[
        \im(0 \to A \cap B) = 0 = \ker \iota, 
    \]
    and 
    \[
        \im \iota = A \cap B = \ker \pi 
    \]
    since the $A \cap B \subseteq A$ go to $0$ modulo $B$, 
    and 
    \[
        \im \pi = \frac{A+B}{B} = \ker (A + B/B \to 0), 
    \]
    since $\pi$ is surjective: 
    take $(a+b) + B \in (A+B)/B$, then 
    \[
        (a+b) + B = a + B 
    \]
    since $b \in B$, which is mapped to by $a \in A$. 

    Thus 
    \[
        0 \to A \cap B \overset\iota\to A \overset\pi\to 
        \frac{A+B}{B} \to 0
    \]
    is exact, reaffirming 
    the fact that, for exact sequence 
    \[
        0 \to A \to B \to C \to 0 
    \]
    we have 
    \[
        C \cong \frac{B}{\im(A \to B)}. 
    \]

    Another natural route is to look at 
    $\psi : A \oplus B \to A + B$ by 
    \[
        \psi(a, b) = a+b. 
    \] 
    Then the kernel forces
    \begin{align*}
        a + b &= 0 \\ 
        a &= -b 
    \end{align*}
    so $a,b \in A \cap B$. This points to the natural map 
    $\varphi : A \cap B \to A \oplus B$ by 
    \[
        \varphi(x) = (x,-x). 
    \]
    Then the sequence 
    \[
        0 \to A \cap B \overset\varphi\to A \oplus B 
        \overset\psi\to A + B \to 0
    \]
    is exact. 

    This is not hard to prove as 
    \[
        \im(0 \to A\cap B) = 0 = \ker\varphi 
    \]
    since $(x,-x) = 0$ iff $x = 0$. Then 
    \[
        \im\varphi = \{(x,-x) \bigm| x \in A \cap B\} 
        = \ker \psi 
    \]
    by the earlier argument. 
    Lastly 
    \[
        \im \psi = A + B = \ker (A + B \to 0)   
    \]
    since $\psi$ is surjective (for $a+b \in A+B$ there 
    is $(a,b) \in A \oplus B$). 
\end{proof}

\begin{prob}
    Let $M$ be an $R$-module and let 
    $r \in R$. Consider only 
    \[
        M, \ \Ann_M(r) = \{m \in M \bigm| rm = 0\}, 
        \ M/rM. 
    \]

    There is a very natural endomorphism 
    \[
        M \to M 
    \]
    associated with $r$. 

    Discover an exact sequence involving 
    \[
        \Ann_M(r), \ M, \ M, \ M/rM.
    \]
\end{prob}
\begin{proof}[Solution and proof]
    A natural endomorphism on $M$ associated 
    with $r$ could be $\rho : M \to M$ by 
    \[
        \rho(m) = rm. 
    \] 
    Since modules are closed under multiplication 
    from the ring, this is well defined. 
    It is not terribly hard to see that it is 
    indeed a homomorphism since 
    \begin{align*}
        \rho(am_1 + m_2) 
        &= r(am_1 + m_2) \\ 
        &= ram_1 + rm_2 \\ 
        &= a(rm_1) + rm_2 \\ 
        &= a\rho(m_1) + \rho(m_2). 
    \end{align*}

    This map need not be injective, and 
    it need not be surjective either. 
    Take for instance $R$ to be $\mathbb Z$ and 
    $M$ to be $\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z$ with $r = 2$. 
    Then the image does not include $\overline 1$. 
    
    The first useful fact is that for 
    $m \in \Ann_M(r) \subseteq M$ we have 
    $m \overset\rho\mapsto 0$. 

    Which gives a candidate:
    \[
        0 \to \Ann_M(r) \overset\iota\to M 
        \overset\rho\to M \overset\pi\to M/rM \to 0. 
    \]

    Let $\iota : \Ann_M(r) \to M$ be the inlcusion map, 
    which is well-defined since 
    $\Ann_M(r) \subseteq M$. 

    Then let $\pi : M \to M/rM$ be the projection map 
    \[
        m \overset\pi\mapsto \overline m. 
    \]

    Then 
    \[
        \im(0\to\Ann_M(r)) = 0_{\Ann_M(r)}, 
    \]
    but $\Ann_M(r) \subseteq M$ so 
    \[
        0_{\Ann_M(r)} = 0_M, 
    \]
    hence 
    \[
        \im(0\to\Ann_M(r)) = \ker\iota. 
    \]
    Next, we have that 
    \[
        \im\iota = \Ann_M(r). 
    \]
    By definition, for $m \in M$, 
    \[
        mr = \rho(m) = 0 
    \]
    if and only if 
    \[
        m \in \Ann_M(r), 
    \]
    so 
    \[
        \ker\rho = \Ann_M(r) = \im\iota. 
    \]
    Next 
    \[
        \im\rho  
    \]
    is $rM = \{rm \bigm| m \in M\}$, 
    but this is just kernel $\ker \pi$ 
    since $x = 0$ in $M/rM$ if and only if 
    $x \in rM$ if and only if 
    \[
        x = rm, \ m \in M. 
    \]     
    Thus 
    \[
        \im\rho = \ker\pi. 
    \]
    Lastly, 
    \[
        \im\pi = M/rM 
    \]
    as the projection is always surjective sicne 
    there is at least one representative 
    $m \in M$ for each residue class modulo $rM$. 
    
    Now we take a look at a concrete 
    example with $R = \mathbb Z$, $M = \mathbb Z/12\mathbb Z$ 
    and $r = 8$. 

    Then 
    \[
        \Ann_M(8) = \{\overline m \in \mathbb Z/12\mathbb Z 
        \bigm| m \cdot 8 = n \cdot 12, \ n \in 
        \mathbb Z\}. 
    \]. 
    The annihilators (worked out concretely 
    further down) are then  
    \[
        \overline 0, \ \overline 3, \ \overline 6, \ \overline 9,
    \]
    meaning 
    \[
        \Ann_M(8) = (3). 
    \]

    Then 
    \[
        8M
    \] 
    is the residue classes modulo $12$ 
    after being multiplied by $8 = 2\cdot 2 \cdot 2$.
    Concretely, 
    \begin{align*}
        8 \cdot \overline 0  &= \overline 0 \\ 
        8 \cdot \overline 1  &= \overline 8 \\ 
        8 \cdot \overline 2  &= \overline 4 \\ 
        8 \cdot \overline 3  &= \overline 0 \\ 
        8 \cdot \overline 4  &= \overline 8 \\ 
        8 \cdot \overline 5  &= \overline 4 \\ 
        8 \cdot \overline 6  &= \overline 0 \\ 
        8 \cdot \overline 7  &= \overline 8 \\ 
        8 \cdot \overline 8  &= \overline 4 \\ 
        8 \cdot \overline 9  &= \overline 0 \\ 
        8 \cdot \overline 10 &= \overline 8 \\ 
        8 \cdot \overline 11 &= \overline 4, 
    \end{align*} 
    meaning 
    \[
        8M = (4)
    \]
    in this scenario.

    Note: 
    We could also have worked out 
    the annihilator by observing that 
    \[
        8m \cong 0 (\mod 12) 
    \]
    if and only if 
    \[
        12 \mid 8m 
    \]
    if and only if 
    \[
        3 \mid 2m 
    \]
    if and only if (because $2$ and $3$ are coprime) 
    \[
        3 \mid m. 
    \]

    For the maps we have that the 
    $0$ map has image $\overline 0$, 
    with the kernel of the inclusion from 
    the annihilator being $\overline 0$ as well. 

    Furthermore the image of the inclusion, which 
    I will here also denote $\iota$ is itself 
    the annihilator 
    \[
        (3). 
    \] 
    The kernel of the endomorphism sending 
    $m \mapsto 8m$ is the 
    elements for which $8m$ is $0$, namely 
    \[
        \overline 0, \ \overline 3, \ \overline 6, \ \overline 9.  
    \]

    The image of the endomorphism is 
    \[
        8M = (4) 
    \]
    and the kernel of the 
    projection $\pi$ is $8M$. 
    The image of the projection is the whole 
    module $M/8M$ so the last term 
    \[
        \overset\pi\to M/8M \to 0
    \]
    is exact. 

    We have that 
    \[
        \frac{\mathbb Z/12\mathbb Z}{(4)}
        \cong \mathbb Z/4\mathbb Z. 
    \] 
    You can show this by 
    taking the surjective homomorphism 
    $\varphi : \mathbb Z/12\mathbb Z \to (4)$ 
    by 
    \[
        \varphi(\overline n_{12}) = \overline n_{4}
    \]
    and applying the first isomorphism theorem. 

    For well-definedness we observe that 
    if $n_1 \equiv n_2$ modulo $12$ then, since $4 \mid 12$, 
    we have that $n_1 \equiv n_2$ modulo $4$. 

    Is is a homomorphism since 
    \begin{align*}
        \varphi(r\overline n_{12} + \overline m_{12})

        \overline 0 \mapsfrom \overline 0, 
        \ \overline 1 \mapsfrom \overline 1, 
        \ \overline 2 \mapsfrom \overline 2, \
        \ \overline 3 \mapsfrom \overline 3.  
    \]
    Note: We surpress the earlier used 
    subscripts denoting which modulo we're taking, but 
    the point is it's obvious.

    Then we get an exact sequence

        0 \to (3) \overset\iota\to \mathbb Z/12\mathbb Z 
        \overset{\times8}{\to} \mathbb Z/12\mathbb Z 
        \overset{\pi \circ \varphi}\to \mathbb Z/4 
        \mathbb Z \to 0.  
    \]
